# Underwater physics: a coherent direction for the spike

This is a design for the experimental branch, not a claim that CrabScape already simulates a real ocean. The goal is a world that looks settled and alive, responds credibly to creatures, and remains predictable enough to tune, debug, and run on a phone. The material simulation is part of gameplay: walking, digging, swimming, currents, vegetation, and creatures should respond to the same world state.

## The story

Water fills the space between solid things and has a local velocity. Gravity pulls sediment down; water moving relative to a grain drags on it. A grain's size, density, and cohesion determine how readily it falls, sticks to neighbors, or is carried away. A creature can transfer force into water and into whatever its legs or body touch. The water then affects other grains and plants nearby. A clump can break apart, and its lightest fine material can drift away as silt. The bottom is neither frozen nor perpetually rearranged by a gentle background current: it is the outcome of deposits, past motion, and continuing disturbances.

A crab has legs rather than a rigid box that bulldozes the floor. It seeks support over a footprint, steps over low obstacles, and uses effort to move loose material. Pebbles should be more resistant than fine sand because they are larger and heavier; they are still individual loose objects, not one immovable wall. Jumping pushes down and outward into the water and the bed. Digging transfers a bounded force into sediment; rock and loads beyond that force stay put. Being buried should not let a creature fly in open water.

Kelp is rooted and buoyant, with flexible connected sections. Moving water drags on it, while its root, buoyancy, and internal tension keep it together. An animal can climb the same moving stalk it sees. All creatures should eventually interact with these same water and substrate rules, scaled by their size and locomotion.

## What the spike actually does

- The opening has a 640 × 360 grid, four world units per cell, advanced at 30 Hz inside the 120 Hz deterministic game simulation. Water occupies empty cells; rock, sand, and mud occupy solid cells. The field is local, not the whole world.
- The water field is a velocity approximation: local diffusion, a gentle time- and depth-varying feed, obstacle deflection, and disturbances from animals and moving grains. It has no pressure solve or proof of incompressibility, momentum conservation, or real-world units. A separate, stateless current drives distant water and the upper stalk; the boundary between the two is not coupled yet.
- Sand and mud share particle movement, gravity, current, and bounded pushing. Their parameters include a density distribution, erosion threshold, cohesion, suspension time, and push cost. Mud cohesion is an approximation: neighbors slow separation and retain edges; unsupported grains still fall. Fine silt sprites are a visual, lightweight detail, not additional conserved material. Sand, mud, and rock cells themselves are conserved by swaps.
- Pebbles in the opening are owned 4 × 4 cell aggregates. Each has one identity and moves its occupied footprint atomically; the same cells make up its collision footprint. They are heavier to push than sand and affect walking's granular resistance. Walking nudges an exposed pebble; digging applies a stronger impulse and can push a short chain of pebbles through loose sediment. A bounded plan checks the chain before moving it, and displaced sediment swaps into the vacated cells. During downward digging, the claws also sweep pebbles beside the crab upward to make space for the displaced material. This CA prototype translates on the grid without rotation; longer or more tightly packed chains can still exceed the force budget. The impulses, water displacement, and contact forces are approximations.
- When loose sediment is trapped against a pebble rim, digging can carry a grain along a bounded path of open water through the crab's working space and deposit it outside the body. The operation preserves the grain and cannot cross rock or pebbles. This is an approximation of a claw scoop, not a granular pressure solver; available paths and the force budget still limit it.
- The crab and on-map animals sample a supported surface over a footprint, filtering isolated grains. The crab can step up and tilt on slopes, brush loose grains, and apply bounded dig pushes. Pebble resistance is an explicit approximation of the work of moving the material underfoot, not a resolved leg-by-leg friction solver. Rock and existing level objects remain solid. On-map animals use surface sampling and stir the field but do not yet share all crab/substrate interactions.
- Digging speed responds to a smoothed estimate of how hard the nearby material is to rearrange: material load and cohesion ahead, plus the amount of nearby space beside and behind the crab. Each pebble aggregate is counted once. An open escape route reduces resistance; confinement increases it. This local estimate does not yet measure full flow connectivity, pressure, or the exact work of all displaced material. Solid rock remains an obstruction.
- A jump creates a downward-and-outward water impulse and can entrain light surface grains. Swimming and other movement make wakes. These are local gameplay impulses, not equal-and-opposite momentum accounted for across a full fluid and body solver.
- The initial opening is made from the existing level and loose deposits, then simulated for five minutes without creatures. A checked-in snapshot loads that result quickly; thereafter the world continues to evolve. It is a warm start, not a promise that every current has an equilibrium or that terrain returns to a fixed shape. The bake must be regenerated after changes to initial terrain or sediment rules.
- The fluid, grid kelp, cells, and snapshot use floating point where appropriate. They are repeatable within the tested environment, but full cross-platform bit-identical determinism is not established. Terrain resets on reload; co-op and terrain persistence are not implemented for this experiment.

## A more unified model

Treat each interaction as a transfer between named participants, using one vocabulary instead of unrelated special cases:

1. **Material state.** Each object or grain has a material, mass or density, size, velocity, and cohesion/friction parameters. Parameters describe a family of materials; seeded variation gives individual grains differences. Water has velocity and density; rock has fixed geometry unless explicitly destructible.
2. **Forces and contacts.** Gravity, buoyancy, drag from relative water motion, contact, friction, and cohesive bonds produce bounded impulses. A creature's muscles supply a limited impulse budget. Apply the corresponding reaction to the water or substrate where feasible. Keep contact with anchored rock distinct from contact with independent sediment.
3. **Integration.** At a fixed tick, collect inputs and impulses, resolve contacts and grain transfers in a stable order, update water and vegetation, and publish the resulting state. Deterministic ordering and seeded variation matter more than visual update order. Document any deliberate energy damping or clamping.
4. **Presentation.** Draw the state without changing it. Particle color and apparent size follow the particle. Small silt can be translucent and sub-cell; visual-only particles must not create gameplay mass. Render quality and camera zoom must not change the authoritative result.

This is a target architecture, not a requirement to implement a full Navier–Stokes or molecular sediment solver. Approximate rules are welcome when they have a clear cause, a bounded effect, and a testable relationship to the same material and force parameters. Prefer a small number of shared transfer rules over separate hidden speed multipliers for each animal or material.

### Pebble representation

The first prototype uses an **aggregate of CA cells**: each cell has the same pebble ID, and all cells belonging to that pebble move together. Adjacent cells of different pebbles remain separate, even when they touch. The pebble's occupied cells form its collision footprint, and the push budget depends on its cell count. A proposed push plans a short chain before moving it, and each moved aggregate exchanges its leading edge with loose sediment or water. Stable object and cell ordering resolve competing moves. The current bounded force model is an approximation and needs more varied pile and boundary tests.

This is a grid-based rigid aggregate, rather than relying only on local cohesion. Local bonds make sense for deformable mud or a clump that can break, but strong bonds alone do not automatically make a rigid pebble: independently updated cells can stretch, tear, merge with a neighbor, or depend on update order. An explicitly owned aggregate avoids those problems, at the cost of managing object identity, whole-footprint movement, and interactions at the boundary. A first version can omit rotation or use a few discrete orientations, but that limitation needs a feel test: pebble rolling and jamming may look wrong without it. Fracture, if added, should explicitly split an aggregate rather than silently create or lose cells.

The main alternative is a small pool of separate rigid pebbles that overlap or reserve cells in the existing grid and exchange impulses with sand and water. It naturally represents size, rotation, and contacts, but adds body bookkeeping, contact detection, and a coupling problem at the grid boundary. These alternatives are not necessarily exclusive: a rigid body can itself have a CA-cell footprint. Noita's creators describe combining a falling-sand simulation with destructible rigid-body physics in their [GDC talk](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025695/Exploring-the-Tech-and-Design); that establishes a useful precedent, not a performance result for this game.

**Recommended next spike:** improve the aggregate prototype's packed contacts, then compare it with a small rigid-body prototype using the same pebble bed and interactions. Measure step and render time, memory, and worst-frame cost on desktop and a representative phone at several pebble counts, with both calm and disturbed piles. Compare conservation, stable resting piles, rolling or sliding, stacking, force transfer, digging, fluid coupling, and repeatability. Neither approach can be declared cheaper for CrabScape without those measurements.

### Surface and locomotion

Keep a shared support query for the player and ground animals. It should describe contact height, normal, supporting materials, and confidence over the actual foot span; isolated grains do not create a floor. Legs can lift over obstacles shorter than the animal, and ordinary walking can move loose material without silently digging a tunnel. Slopes and transitions must preserve smooth horizontal control and jump takeoff. Digging should work only while there is material to push against. Validate with controlled flat, sloped, granular, and obstructed scenes and with repeated reversals.

### Water, sediment, and vegetation

Move toward a locally coupled velocity/pressure approximation with explicit boundary conditions and controllable dissipation. A creature stroke, falling grain, or swaying stalk should deposit an impulse into water; water drag should feed back to the object. Measure the numerical loss and cap forces to keep the game stable. Cohesion should depend on local neighbors and material parameters, not freeze an authored layer. Fine material may separate and be advected; distinguish mass-carrying grains from visual silt. Rooted kelp should bend along its length and be driven by the same local water that moves nearby grains.

### Whole world, time, and persistence

Keep simulation independent of camera and zoom. Partition the world into chunks with stable IDs, deterministic updates, saved state, and explicit boundary transfers. A sleeping chunk may advance analytically only for state whose evolution and external influences can actually be summarized. For disturbed sediment and fluids, exact catch-up requires replaying the relevant history and boundary events; a cheaper coarse approximation must be explicitly defined and tested. Keep active creatures and their interactions in the activation rules. Measure worst-case wake-up time, memory, and work per frame before choosing a chunk size or promising phone performance.

## Checks for every iteration

- Sand, mud, and pebble material is conserved across simulation and pushing; rock never moves from a loose-grain push. Visual silt cannot create collision or support.
- Stronger pushes displace at least as much comparable loose material; denser/cohesive material resists more under otherwise identical conditions. Loose pebble beds remain traversable, and digging can still move their grains.
- Jumping, digging, slopes, sparse overhead grains, and repeated direction changes do not cause stalls, invisible floors, or flight. A real ceiling still blocks upward movement.
- The same inputs and seed produce the same state on repeated runs. Add native/WebAssembly comparisons before claiming cross-target determinism, plus chunk sleep/wake and boundary tests before enabling dormant simulation.
- Run timed mobile and desktop measurements for stepping, drawing, particle counts, memory, startup, and worst-case wake-up. The visible game is the final feel test; a passing numerical test does not prove it feels good.
